\vspace{4pt}
\noindent
PIVTOOLs processes 1\,MP image pairs at \textbf{81\,Hz} (64$\to$32, 2-pass) or \textbf{41\,Hz} (64$\to$32$\to$16, 3-pass) on a single worker with 10~OMP threads---comfortably exceeding the 10--15\,Hz acquisition rate of most double-pulsed PIV cameras. Processing time scales linearly with pixel count: 4\,MP images take 4.1--4.3$\times$ longer (50\,ms and 105\,ms per pair respectively). Batch size has no effect on per-pair throughput---the CPU is already saturated on a single pair at 10~threads. Save I/O is negligible when uncompressed (${\sim}$1\,ms/pair), but enabling zlib compression costs $12\times$ more I/O time while reducing file size 4--6$\times$; the recommended default is uncompressed for throughput, compressed only for archival. For distributed processing, independent Dask workers scale $2\times$ more efficiently than adding threads to a single worker (62\% vs 27\% parallel efficiency at 20~cores), because the bottleneck is memory bandwidth rather than compute---the optimal allocation is 2--4~OMP threads per worker with remaining cores as additional workers. On a 20-core workstation with 10~workers $\times$ 2~threads, a 4000-pair 4\,MP dataset completes in ${\sim}$13~minutes; at 1\,MP with 2~passes, a single worker finishes the same dataset in under 1~minute.

\section{How the Pipeline Works}
% ======================================================================

Each image pair is processed through multi-grid refinement:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Pass~1 (64\,px windows):} Cross-correlate the raw images for a coarse velocity estimate.
\item \textbf{Pass~2 (32\,px windows):} Warp both images using Pass~1's predictor, then cross-correlate on a $2\times$ finer grid.
\item \textbf{Pass~3 (16\,px, optional):} Warp using Pass~2, correlate on the $4\times$ finer grid.
\end{enumerate}

After each pass, median outlier detection removes spurious vectors and local median gap filling replaces them. Image warping uses a custom C~library (\texttt{libfusedwarp}) that fuses predictor upsampling, symmetric coordinate mapping, and bicubic interpolation into a single OpenMP-parallel loop, avoiding intermediate array allocations.

\textbf{Why does warping saturate?} The fused warp kernel touches every pixel in both images (bicubic $4\times4$ stencil per output pixel). At 4\,MP, this means ${\sim}$33 million interpolation lookups. The 19\,ms cost is memory-bandwidth limited---the same whether the grid is 127$^2$ or 255$^2$, because the \emph{image} size, not the \emph{grid} size, determines warp cost.

\textbf{Why does cross-correlation grow?} The number of FFT windows scales with the grid: 255$^2 = 65\,025$ windows vs 127$^2 = 16\,129$. Each window requires a forward-FFT pair, complex multiply, and inverse FFT---all O($W^2 \log W$) per window. The $4\times$ window count drives the 2.7$\times$ increase from Pass~2 to Pass~3 (partial overlap reduces the scaling below $4\times$).

\section{Save I/O Trade-offs}
\label{sec:saveio}
% ======================================================================

PIVTOOLs offers two save axes: \textbf{mode} (full: 11 fields vs minimal: 3 fields) and \textbf{compression} (zlib on/off). These interact to produce large differences in both I/O latency and disk footprint.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}llrrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Mode} & \textbf{Compress} & \textbf{Save} & \textbf{File} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{4000 pairs}} \\
 & & \textbf{(ms)} & \textbf{(KB)} & \textbf{Disk} & \textbf{Time} \\
\midrule
Full     & Yes & 24.9 & 173  & 676\,MB & 100\,s \\
Full     & No  & 2.0  & 986  & 3.9\,GB & 8\,s \\
Minimal  & Yes & 11.4 & 80   & 312\,MB & 46\,s \\
Minimal  & No  & \textbf{1.0}  & 334  & 1.3\,GB & \textbf{4\,s} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{Save overhead per pair (4\,MP, 64$\to$32, 10 threads). Full mode saves all diagnostic fields (peak height, predictor, sigmas). Minimal saves only \texttt{ux}, \texttt{uy}, \texttt{b\_mask}---sufficient for most analyses.}
\end{center}

\textbf{Key observations:}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Compression dominates save cost.} Turning on zlib multiplies save time by $11\text{--}12\times$ (from 1--2\,ms to 11--25\,ms). For a 4000-pair dataset, this is the difference between 4\,s and 100\,s of pure I/O.

\item \textbf{Minimal mode halves file size.} Compressed: 80\,KB vs 173\,KB ($2.2\times$). Uncompressed: 334\,KB vs 986\,KB ($3.0\times$). The saving is less than the $3.7\times$ field count ratio (11/3) because \texttt{ux}/\texttt{uy} are the largest fields in both modes.

\item \textbf{Compression ratio.} Full mode compresses $5.7\times$ (986$\to$173\,KB); minimal compresses $4.2\times$ (334$\to$80\,KB). Velocity fields compress well because neighbouring vectors are correlated.

\item \textbf{Recommended defaults.} For maximum throughput (real-time, HPC): \texttt{minimal + uncompressed} ($<$2\% overhead). For archival storage: \texttt{minimal + compressed} (312\,MB per 4000 pairs). Switch to \texttt{full} only when diagnostic fields are needed for debugging convergence or ensemble fitting quality.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why Workers Beat Threads}

At 20 physical cores, the choice is between allocating cores as threads within one worker or as independent workers:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Configuration} & \textbf{ms/pair} & \textbf{Hz} & \textbf{Efficiency} \\
\midrule
1~worker $\times$ 20~threads  & 403 & 2.5 & 27\% \\
10~workers $\times$ 2~threads & 197 & 5.1 & 62\% \\
\addlinespace[2pt]
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{Worker advantage:}} & \textbf{2.0$\times$} & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\captionof{table}{Same 20 cores, two strategies. Workers deliver $2\times$ the throughput because each worker has its own memory bus bandwidth allocation and cache hierarchy. Threading forces 20 threads to compete for a single worker's memory subsystem.}
\end{center}

\textbf{The fundamental bottleneck is memory bandwidth, not compute.} Cross-correlation and image warping stream large arrays through the cache hierarchy. A single worker with 20~threads saturates one NUMA domain's bandwidth, leaving threads idle-waiting on cache misses. Multiple workers, each with fewer threads, distribute the memory pressure across the full system.

\textbf{Amdahl comparison:} The thread serial fraction ($f{=}14.2\%$) is $2\times$ the worker serial fraction ($f{=}6.8\%$). This is not true serial code---it reflects shared-memory contention. Workers avoid this by operating on independent data in separate address spaces.

\textbf{Optimal allocation rule:} 2--4 threads per worker (enough for intra-pair FFT parallelism), with remaining cores allocated as additional workers. On a 20-core machine: $10\text{w} \times 2\text{t}$ or $5\text{w} \times 4\text{t}$. On a 40-core HPC node: $20\text{w} \times 2\text{t}$.

\textbf{Bare correlator vs Dask pipeline:} The Dask pipeline at $10\text{w} \times 2\text{t}$ processes 4\,MP 3-pass data at 5.1\,Hz (197\,ms/pair). The bare correlator at 10~threads does 9.5\,Hz (105\,ms/pair)---$1.9\times$ faster per core-second. The gap reflects Dask serialisation (${\sim}$10--20\,ms/batch), task scheduling, and Python GIL contention during data scattering. For single-worker scenarios (small datasets, prototyping), bypassing Dask and calling the correlator directly recovers this overhead.

\textbf{Scaling projection:} On a 40-core machine with 128\,GB RAM, extrapolating the 62\% parallel efficiency to 20 workers suggests ${\sim}$10\,Hz for 4\,MP 3-pass, reducing the 4000-pair estimate to ${\sim}$7\,minutes.

\textbf{I/O budget (minimal + uncompressed):} Save overhead for 4000 pairs is ${\sim}$4\,s ($<$1\% of total wall time). Disk: 1.3\,GB per camera---negligible on modern storage. If archiving with compression, add ${\sim}$46\,s per camera.

\section{Key Takeaways}
% ======================================================================

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]

\item \textbf{81\,Hz at 1\,MP} (64$\to$32) or \textbf{41\,Hz} (64$\to$32$\to$16) on a single worker with 10 threads. Including minimal saves: ${\sim}$75\,Hz and ${\sim}$39\,Hz. Doubling to 20~threads does not help---the working set fits in cache and memory bandwidth is already saturated at~10.

\item \textbf{20\,Hz at 4\,MP} (64$\to$32) or \textbf{10\,Hz} (64$\to$32$\to$16). Fast enough for many production workflows without parallelisation.

\item \textbf{1.8$\times$ faster than previously reported.} The earlier 21--201\,ms figures included save overhead. Isolating compute reveals 12--105\,ms per pair.

\item \textbf{Processing scales linearly with pixel count.} 4.2$\times$ pixels $\to$ 4.1--4.3$\times$ time. Resolution is the strongest performance lever.

\item \textbf{Batch size does not matter} with 10 OMP threads---the CPU is already saturated on a single pair.

\item \textbf{Save compression costs $12\times$ more time} than uncompressed writes, while reducing file size $4\text{--}6\times$. Default to uncompressed for throughput; compress only for archival.

\item \textbf{Workers scale $2\times$ better than threads} at the same core count (62\% vs 27\% efficiency at 20 cores). Memory bandwidth, not compute, is the bottleneck.

\item \textbf{Optimal allocation: 2--4 threads per worker.} On a 20-core machine: 10~workers $\times$ 2~threads = 5.1\,Hz at 4\,MP 3-pass.

\item \textbf{4000 pairs in 3--13 minutes} depending on resolution, passes, and parallelisation. 1\,MP 2-pass completes in under 1~minute on a single worker.

\item \textbf{Real-time processing is achievable} at 1\,MP: 75\,Hz including saves exceeds the 10--15\,Hz acquisition rate of most double-pulsed PIV cameras by $5\times$.
\end{enumerate}
